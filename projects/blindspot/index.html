The Blind Spot Index

Rooftop solar is invisible to the grid operator. This measures how big a surprise that becomes when a cloud rolls in. California, 2025.

Start here

When solar panels on a home or a warehouse make electricity, that power lands on the customer's side of the electric meter. Some of it is used on site; the surplus flows back out and serves a neighbor. Either way the grid operator never measures the generation itself. All it sees is the net demand left over, once the panels have covered whatever they covered. It cannot tell the difference between a quiet hour and a sunny one.

Then a cloud bank moves in. Solar output can fall by most of its value within an hour, but the lights, servers, and air conditioners stay on, so that load can land back on the grid all at once. On the operator's screens nothing caused it. Demand simply jumps. That's the blind spot of predicting demand. As more and more behind-the-meter solar output is generated, due to solar power generation becoming cheaper and more convenient, this fluctuation will increase.

This page sizes that blind spot for California's three big utility territories. A physics estimate works forward from how much rooftop solar is installed and how much sunlight actually hit it, hour by hour, using no demand data at all. A statistical check then works backward from real, metered demand to see whether it moves with cloud cover the way the physics says it should. It does, in all three territories.

The numbers, defined

Blind Spot Index
The largest one-hour drop in estimated rooftop solar output during 2025, divided by that territory's highest measured demand of the year. A reading of 25% means the worst single-hour disappearance of hidden generation was worth a quarter of the territory's busiest hour. The drop and the peak are the two worst hours of the year for each measure, not necessarily the same hour: this sizes one against the other, it doesn't describe one moment.
Hidden share at midday
Between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m., the share of all electricity being consumed that comes from rooftop solar and so never appears in demand data. The denominator is gross load: metered demand plus the estimated rooftop generation offsetting it. Whether a given kilowatt-hour was used on site or exported to a neighbor doesn't change this. Both reduce metered demand by the same amount, and the neighbor is inside the territory either way.
Biggest one-hour drop
The raw megawatt version of the Blind Spot Index, and the hour it happened. For scale, a large natural-gas plant is roughly 500 MW, so these are multi-plant swings.
Cloud-driven swing days
Days in 2025 with at least one hour where rooftop output fell by 5% or more of peak demand and the sun was still well up. Sunset produces a much larger drop every single evening, but it arrives on schedule and operators plan around it. Only the unscheduled kind counts here.
Demand response to cloud cover
The statistical check: how many megawatts metered demand actually rises when cloud cover increases by 10 percentage points against a clear sky, holding temperature, weekends, and holidays fixed. This is measured from real demand, with no reference to the physics estimate, which is what makes it independent confirmation rather than a restatement. R² is how much of the hour-to-hour variation in demand the model accounts for overall.
Rooftop solar installed
Total behind-the-meter capacity in the territory, covering both homes and commercial buildings. A commercial rooftop array offsetting its own building's draw is exactly as invisible to the operator as a homeowner's, so both belong in the same number.

The three territories

PG&E
Pacific Gas & Electric: Northern and Central California.
SCE
Southern California Edison: most of Southern California, excluding San Diego.
SDG&E
San Diego Gas & Electric: San Diego and southern Orange County.

Together these make up most of CAISO, the operator of California's grid. They are kept separate because CAISO publishes real hourly demand for each one, which means three independent tests of the idea instead of one statewide average. "All regions" adds them up hour by hour and recomputes from the combined total, so it reflects a genuine coincident peak and a genuine coincident swing rather than an average of the three.

Where the data comes from, and what it can't tell you

Installed capacity is from Berkeley Lab's Tracking the Sun, built from utility interconnection filings. Sunlight is satellite-derived from NREL's NSRDB. Demand is CAISO's own published actuals, per territory. All figures cover 2025.

Two limits worth stating plainly. First, rooftop generation here is a modeled figure, not a metered one. Surplus sent back to the grid does get metered, for billing, but the share consumed on site never does, so nobody records total output. That is the premise of the project, and it is why the physics estimate is checked against real demand rather than against a direct measurement of the generation itself. There isn't one. Second, each territory's weather comes from a single satellite grid point, so the model treats one cloud as shading every panel in the territory at once. Real fleets are spread over hundreds of miles and never all go dark together, which means the single-hour swings here should be read as an upper bound on how bad a cloud event gets, not as a typical one. The day-to-day and midday shares, which don't hinge on one extreme hour, are the more robust numbers on this page.
